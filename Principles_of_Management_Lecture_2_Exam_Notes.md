## 2. Meaning and Definition of Management

### 2.1 Meaning
**Management** is the process of working with and through people and other resources to achieve organisational objectives effectively and efficiently.

In simple words, management involves deciding what must be done, arranging people and resources, guiding employees, and checking that work is completed as intended.

### 2.2 Exam-ready definition
A commonly used definition highlighted in the lecture is:

> **“Management is the art of getting things done through others.”**

This definition emphasises that managers achieve results by coordinating and directing the work of other people rather than doing every task themselves.

**Additional clarification:** A fuller modern explanation also includes efficiency, effectiveness, planning, organising, staffing, directing, and controlling.

### 2.3 Management as a process
The lecture describes management as a **process**, meaning a series of interrelated functions. The functions named in the subtitles are:
1. **Planning** — deciding in advance what is to be done and how.
2. **Organising** — arranging activities and resources.
3. **Staffing** — obtaining and placing suitable people in jobs.
4. **Directing** — guiding, leading, communicating with, and motivating employees.
5. **Controlling** — comparing actual performance with plans and correcting deviations.

The lecture also stresses **coordination**: bringing different people and activities together so that they work towards a common objective.

### Example
A college planning an annual event must decide the budget and schedule (planning), allocate duties and resources (organising), appoint student volunteers (staffing), guide the teams (directing), and check progress against the schedule (controlling). Coordination keeps all teams working together.

## 3. Importance of Management

The lecturer explains five main points.

### 3.1 Helps achieve group objectives

**Explanation:** An organisation brings people together to achieve a shared objective. Management combines human resources (employees and their skills) with non-human resources (such as machinery, raw materials, money, and technology) in a systematic way. This helps the organisation achieve its common goals.

Neither people nor machinery alone are sufficient in many organisational activities: employees operate, supervise, and make decisions about the use of resources.

**Example:** In a manufacturing company, management coordinates workers, machines, raw materials, and production schedules to meet the production target.

**Exam-ready point:** Management helps achieve group objectives by integrating and coordinating human and non-human resources towards a common goal.

### 3.2 Increases efficiency

**Explanation:** The lecture connects good management with producing more output while using fewer resources and keeping costs under control. Management uses planning, organising, staffing, directing, and controlling to improve the use of resources.

- **Efficiency** concerns the relationship between inputs and outputs: using resources economically and reducing waste.
- **Effectiveness** means achieving the intended objective.

**Additional clarification:** An organisation should aim to be both efficient and effective. Producing a large quantity of the wrong product may be efficient in one narrow sense but is not effective if it does not meet the objective.

**Example:** A factory improves its schedule and reduces material wastage while maintaining product quality.

**Exam-ready point:** Management increases efficiency by improving resource utilisation, reducing unnecessary costs and waste, and helping produce more output from available inputs.

### 3.3 Creates a dynamic organisation

**Meaning:** A dynamic organisation can adapt to changes in its environment rather than remaining fixed in one way of working.

**Explanation:** The lecturer notes that business conditions, competition, and customer demands change. Organisations need management to respond to these changes through planning, organising resources, hiring or training suitable employees, motivating staff, and controlling implementation.

**Example from the lecture:** During the shift from offline to online activities, some educational institutions and companies moved many activities online. The lecturer uses this as an example of an organisation adapting to changing conditions.

**Exam-ready point:** Management helps create a dynamic organisation by enabling it to recognise changes and adapt its plans, people, processes, and resources accordingly.

### 3.4 Helps achieve individual objectives

**Explanation:** Employees may join an organisation for different reasons. The lecturer gives examples such as financial needs, gaining experience, and other personal aims. Management helps employees work towards appropriate individual objectives through training, motivation, assigning responsibilities, and providing direction.

**Important distinction:** Individual and organisational objectives may differ, but effective management tries to align them where possible.

**Example:** An employee who wants to develop technical skills may receive suitable training while contributing to the organisation's work.

**Exam-ready point:** Management helps employees achieve individual objectives by providing direction, training, motivation, and opportunities to contribute and develop.

### 3.5 Helps develop society

**Explanation:** According to the lecture, good management can benefit society in several ways:
- Creating employment opportunities.
- Supporting the production of quality goods.
- Providing better services.
- Encouraging the adoption of useful technologies.
- Helping organisations use resources productively.

These benefits extend beyond the organisation when its products, services, jobs, and innovations meet social needs.

**Example:** A well-managed service organisation may create jobs, provide reliable services, and introduce technology that improves convenience for customers.

**Exam-ready point:** Management contributes to social development by creating employment, improving goods and services, and supporting productive use of resources and technology.

## 4. Nature of Management: Science or Art?

The lecture discusses the debate over whether management is a science or an art. Its conclusion is that **management has elements of both science and art**.

### 4.1 Management as a science

**Definition:** Science is a systematic body of knowledge developed through observation, study, and testing, which identifies relationships or principles that help explain phenomena.

The lecturer describes science as organised knowledge developed through observation and repeated experiments. The lecture then relates this idea to management.

#### Features of management as a science

1. **Systematic body of knowledge**  
   Management contains organised concepts, principles, and theories. The lecture mentions scientific management and the principles associated with F. W. Taylor as an example of management knowledge developed through study and observation.

2. **Principles developed through observation and study**  
   Management thinkers examine organisational activities and develop principles to guide practice. The lecture refers to observation and repeated experimentation; the precise wording in the subtitles is unclear, so it should not be taken to mean that every management principle was established through controlled experiments.

3. **Not universally applicable in exactly the same way**  
   The lecturer contrasts management with natural sciences such as physics. A natural law such as gravity operates regardless of a manager's preferences. Management principles, however, may need to be adapted to the circumstances, people, culture, and environment of an organisation.

**Conclusion:** Management is often described as a **social science** because it studies people and organisations, whose behaviour is influenced by social and situational factors.

### 4.2 Management as an art

**Definition:** Art is the practical application of knowledge and skill, using judgement and creativity to achieve a desired result.

The lecture explains that art involves applying existing knowledge with personal skill and creativity. It gives examples of artists such as dancers, actors, painters, and writers who use their skills and personal expression.

#### Features of management as an art

1. **Personal skill and creativity**  
   Managers apply management knowledge using their own judgement, experience, communication, and leadership skills.

2. **Practical application of knowledge**  
   Knowing management principles is not enough; a manager must apply them to real situations.

3. **Practice and improvement**  
   Like artists who improve through repeated practice, managers can strengthen their skills through experience, reflection, and learning.

**Example:** Two managers may know the same motivation theory but use different communication methods because their teams and circumstances differ.

### 4.3 Why management is both science and art

Management is science because it has a systematic body of knowledge and principles. It is art because managers must apply that knowledge with judgement, creativity, experience, and skill.

| Basis | Management as science | Management as art |
|---|---|---|
| Main emphasis | Knowledge and principles | Application and skill |
| How it develops | Study, observation, and analysis | Practice, experience, and creativity |
| What a manager needs | Understanding of concepts and evidence | Judgement, communication, leadership, and adaptation |
| Role in management | Helps explain what may work and why | Helps decide how to apply it in a particular situation |

**Exam-ready conclusion:** Management is both a science and an art. Scientific knowledge provides principles and methods, while the art of management lies in applying them effectively to real people and situations.

### Common confusion
Calling management a science does not mean every management principle works identically in every organisation. Calling it an art does not mean management is merely instinct or has no systematic knowledge.

## 5. Levels of Management

### 5.1 Meaning
**Levels of management** are the hierarchical layers of managerial positions in an organisation, arranged from top to bottom according to their authority and responsibilities.

The lecture identifies three levels:
1. Top-level management.
2. Middle-level management.
3. Operational- or lower-level management.

### Diagram: Management hierarchy

```text
        TOP-LEVEL MANAGEMENT
     Board of Directors, CEO, CFO
                  |
                  v
       MIDDLE-LEVEL MANAGEMENT
 Departmental and divisional managers
                  |
                  v
     OPERATIONAL / LOWER MANAGEMENT
       Supervisors and foremen
                  |
                  v
              WORKERS
```

The diagram represents a typical hierarchy. Actual titles and reporting arrangements vary between organisations.

### 5.2 Top-level management

**Typical positions mentioned in the lecture:**
- Board of Directors.
- President and Vice-President.
- Chief Executive Officer (CEO).
- Chief Operating Officer (COO).
- Chief Financial Officer (CFO).

**Main responsibilities:**
- Establishing organisational objectives.
- Developing overall strategies and broad plans.
- Making major decisions and setting policies.
- Considering changes in the business environment and customer demand.
- Providing overall direction to the organisation.

**Explanation:** Top managers make decisions that affect the organisation as a whole. They generally focus on long-term direction and overall performance rather than supervising every worker's daily tasks.

**Example:** The top management of a company decides to enter a new market and approves the broad strategy and resources needed.

**Exam-ready point:** Top-level management determines the organisation's overall objectives, policies, and strategies and provides direction for the organisation as a whole.

### 5.3 Middle-level management

**Typical positions mentioned in the lecture:**
- Departmental or divisional heads.
- Production manager.
- Finance manager.
- Marketing manager.
- Plant superintendent and other managers responsible for departments or divisions.

**Main responsibilities:**
- Interpreting top management's policies and plans.
- Translating broad plans into departmental plans.
- Allocating staff, duties, and responsibilities.
- Implementing policies through departments.
- Guiding, motivating, and coordinating departmental employees.
- Connecting top management with operational management.

**Explanation:** Middle managers turn broad organisational goals into practical plans for their departments. They communicate information both downwards and upwards.

**Example:** If top management sets an annual production target, the production manager prepares departmental schedules, allocates staff, and monitors progress.

**Exam-ready point:** Middle-level management acts as a link between top and lower management and is responsible for interpreting and implementing policies through departmental plans and activities.

### 5.4 Operational- or lower-level management

**Typical positions mentioned in the lecture:**
- Supervisors.
- Foremen.
- Other frontline supervisors who work directly with operating employees.

**Main responsibilities:**
- Supervising workers' day-to-day activities.
- Assigning and explaining tasks.
- Monitoring work quality and output.
- Maintaining discipline and work standards.
- Helping solve workers' immediate problems and referring unresolved issues upwards.
- Ensuring that work is completed according to instructions and schedules.

**Explanation:** Lower-level managers are closest to the operating employees. Their work focuses on daily execution and direct supervision.

**Example:** A production supervisor checks attendance, explains daily tasks, monitors product quality, and reports problems to the production manager.

**Exam-ready point:** Operational-level management directly supervises operating employees and ensures that day-to-day work is performed correctly, efficiently, and according to required standards.

### 5.5 Comparison of the three levels

| Basis | Top level | Middle level | Operational/lower level |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main focus | Organisation as a whole | Department or division | Daily operations |
| Typical positions | Board, CEO, COO, CFO, president | Departmental/divisional managers | Supervisors, foremen |
| Main role | Sets objectives, strategies, and broad policies | Interprets and implements policies | Directly supervises workers |
| Planning horizon | Usually long-term and strategic | Medium-term and departmental | Short-term and operational |
| Contact with workers | Usually indirect | Through departmental structures | Direct and frequent |
| Authority | Broadest | Intermediate | More limited |

### 5.6 How the levels work together

The levels are connected:
- **Top management** decides what the organisation aims to achieve and sets broad policies.
- **Middle management** translates those aims into departmental plans and allocates responsibilities.
- **Lower management** guides employees in carrying out the work.
- Information about progress and problems can travel upwards so that managers can adjust plans.

This is a simplified model; the exact flow depends on the organisation.

## 6. Important Definitions for Revision

1. **Management:** The process of coordinating people and other resources to achieve organisational objectives effectively and efficiently.
2. **Management process:** A series of interrelated managerial functions, commonly including planning, organising, staffing, directing, and controlling.
3. **Coordination:** The integration of activities and efforts so that different people or units work towards common objectives.
4. **Efficiency:** Achieving output with economical use of resources and minimum avoidable waste.
5. **Effectiveness:** The extent to which intended objectives are achieved.
6. **Dynamic organisation:** An organisation that can respond and adapt to changes in its environment.
7. **Science:** A systematic body of knowledge developed through organised study and evidence.
8. **Art:** The practical application of knowledge and skill, using judgement and creativity to achieve a desired result.
9. **Social science:** A field of study concerned with people, groups, institutions, and social behaviour.
10. **Levels of management:** Hierarchical layers of managerial positions with different degrees of authority and responsibility.
11. **Top-level management:** The highest managerial level, responsible for overall objectives, strategy, major policies, and direction.
12. **Middle-level management:** The managerial level that translates broad policies into departmental plans and coordinates implementation.
13. **Operational/lower-level management:** The level that directly supervises operating employees and daily work.

## 7. Comparisons and Distinctions

### 7.1 Efficiency vs effectiveness

| Efficiency | Effectiveness |
|---|---|
| Focuses on how economically resources are used. | Focuses on whether the intended objective is achieved. |
| Concerned with inputs, outputs, cost, and waste. | Concerned with goals and results. |
| Example: reducing material waste during production. | Example: producing the product customers actually need. |

### B. Short-answer questions

**1. Explain any three points showing the importance of management.**

Management is important because:
1. It helps achieve group objectives by coordinating human and non-human resources.
2. It increases efficiency by improving resource utilisation and reducing waste and costs.
3. It creates a dynamic organisation by helping the organisation adapt to changing business conditions.

Other valid points from the lecture are helping employees achieve individual objectives and contributing to social development.

**2. Explain management as a science.**

Management can be considered a science because it has a systematic body of knowledge and principles developed through study and observation. Management thinkers analyse organisational activities and develop concepts that help managers understand and improve performance. However, management principles do not always apply identically in every situation because organisations and people differ. Therefore, management is commonly regarded as a social science rather than an exact natural science.

**3. Explain management as an art.**

Management is an art because managers must apply their knowledge through personal skill, judgement, creativity, and experience. Knowing a theory is not enough; the manager must decide how to use it with a particular team and situation. Managers also improve their abilities through practice and learning.

**4. What is the role of middle-level management?**

Middle-level management acts as a link between top and lower management. It interprets top management's policies, converts broad objectives into departmental plans, assigns responsibilities, coordinates departmental work, motivates employees, and monitors implementation.

**5. Explain the importance of lower-level management.**

Lower-level management directly supervises operating employees. It assigns daily work, checks quality and performance, maintains discipline, helps resolve immediate problems, and ensures that work follows instructions and required standards.

### C. Long-answer questions

**1. Discuss the importance of management.**

**Introduction:** Management is essential because it coordinates people and resources to achieve organisational objectives.

**Importance:**
1. **Achievement of group objectives:** Management combines human resources and non-human resources in a systematic manner so that the organisation can achieve common goals.
2. **Increase in efficiency:** Planning, organising, staffing, directing, and controlling help improve resource utilisation, reduce waste, and control costs.
3. **Creation of a dynamic organisation:** Management helps an organisation respond to changes in competition, technology, and customer demand.
4. **Achievement of individual objectives:** Training, motivation, direction, and suitable responsibility help employees work towards their individual goals while contributing to organisational performance.
5. **Development of society:** Effective management can generate employment, improve goods and services, and support productive use of technology and resources.

**Conclusion:** Management benefits the organisation, its employees, and society by aligning efforts, improving resource use, and helping organisations respond to change.

**2. Is management a science or an art? Explain.**

**Introduction:** Management is often debated as a science or an art. It has features of both.

**Management as science:**
1. It has a systematic body of knowledge.
2. Its principles and theories are developed through study, observation, and analysis.
3. Its principles guide managerial decisions, although they may need to be adapted to different situations.

**Management as art:**
1. It requires personal skill and judgement.
2. Managers apply knowledge creatively to practical problems.
3. Practice and experience help managers improve their performance.

**Conclusion:** Management is both science and art. Science provides knowledge and principles, while art involves applying them skilfully to particular organisational situations.

**3. Explain the three levels of management and their functions.**

**Introduction:** Levels of management are the hierarchical layers of managerial positions in an organisation. They are commonly divided into top, middle, and lower levels.

1. **Top-level management:** Includes positions such as the Board of Directors, CEO, COO, and CFO. It sets overall objectives, strategies, and broad policies and makes major decisions.
2. **Middle-level management:** Includes departmental and divisional managers, such as production, finance, and marketing managers. It interprets policies, prepares departmental plans, allocates responsibilities, and coordinates implementation.
3. **Operational/lower-level management:** Includes supervisors and foremen. It directly guides workers, assigns daily tasks, monitors quality and discipline, and addresses immediate work problems.

**Diagram:**
```text
Top management: overall objectives and strategy
                    ↓
Middle management: departmental plans and implementation
                    ↓
Lower management: daily supervision and execution
                    ↓
Workers: perform operating tasks
```

**Conclusion:** All three levels are interdependent. Top management provides direction, middle management converts direction into plans, and lower management oversees daily execution.

Operational/lower-level management, because it directly supervises daily work.
