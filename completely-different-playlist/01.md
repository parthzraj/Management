## 2.2 Traditional and Modern Definitions

### (a) Traditional definition: Mary Parker Follett
**[From the video]** "Management is the art of **getting things done through others**." The instructor also phrased this as "getting things done for the boss", meaning getting your own work done by other people.

**In simple words:** The manager's job is to get work done by others, by any means.

### (b) Modern definition: Harold Koontz
**[From the video]** Management is the process of **creating and maintaining an internal environment in an enterprise** in which **individuals working together in groups** can perform **efficiently and effectively** to **accomplish group goals**.

**In simple words:** The manager's job is to create an atmosphere and give facilities so that people working in groups can do their work well.

### Key shift in thinking
| Traditional view | Modern view |
|---|---|
| Focus on getting work done *from* others, even by force ("by any means") | Focus on creating a *supportive environment* in which people can perform |
| Manager as boss | Manager as facilitator |

**[From the video]** The instructor stressed that the traditional approach was about "forcefully getting your work done", whereas the modern approach is about giving facilities and creating an environment in which people perform their work.

### Exam-writing version
> **Traditional:** Mary Parker Follett defined management as "the art of getting things done through others."
> **Modern:** Harold Koontz defined it as the process of designing and maintaining an environment in which individuals, working together in groups, efficiently accomplish selected aims. The modern view therefore stresses creating a supportive environment rather than merely getting work done.

**Common confusion:** Follett's definition is not wrong. It is simply narrower. It highlights *who* does the work (others) but not *how* the manager enables them.

## 2.3 Manager, Effectiveness and Efficiency

### Manager
**[From the video]** "The person who directs the efforts of others is a manager." A manager is one who gets the work of others done.

### Effectiveness
**[From the video]** Completion of the assigned task **on time**. If a task given to you is completed on schedule, you are *effective*.
**[Additional clarification]** Often summarised as "doing the *right* things" or achieving the goal.

### Efficiency
**[From the video]** **Optimal utilisation of resources**: using fewer resources (less cost) and getting maximum output.
**[Additional clarification]** Often summarised as "doing things *right*", i.e. getting the most output for the least input.

### Example
**[Additional clarification]** A factory must produce 1,000 units by Friday. If it does, it is **effective**. If it did so using less raw material, less labour and lower cost than expected, it is also **efficient**. A team could finish on time but waste a lot of material: effective but not efficient.

### Exam-writing version
> Effectiveness means completing the assigned task on time (achieving the objective). Efficiency means the optimal utilisation of resources, obtaining maximum output at minimum cost. Management aims to be both effective and efficient.

## 2.4 Functions of Management

**[From the video]** The instructor says managers perform **five functions**. He also mentions another view that adds more.

> **Attribution [unclear in subtitles]:** The names of the authors who propose the five functions are garbled. The text appears to mention Koontz and O'Donnell for the five-function view, and Luther Gulick for POSDCORB. Verify in the video.

### The five functions
**Planning → Organizing → Staffing → Directing → Controlling**

### 1. Planning
**[From the video]** The manager:
- determines **objectives**
- does **forecasting**
- prepares **budgets**
- develops **policies** and **programs**

**In simple words:** Deciding in advance what to do and how to do it.
**Why it comes first:** Every other function depends on knowing the objectives and plans.

### 2. Organizing
**[From the video]**
- **Identification and grouping of activities**: dividing work into groups.
- **Assigning duties** to people.
- **Developing relationships** between people and activities.
- Arranging scattered activities and people so they work together in order.

**In simple words:** Dividing the work, assigning it, and connecting people so the plan can be carried out.

### 3. Staffing
**[From the video]** To perform the activities you need **resources, especially personnel**. The manager decides:
- which task needs which type of person
- what qualifications they need
- **recruitment**, **selection**, **training and development**

**In simple words:** Getting the right people in the right jobs.

### 4. Directing
**[From the video]** The manager:
- **directs and leads** the staff
- **communicates** with them
- **motivates** them
- **supervises** them

**In simple words:** Guiding and inspiring people to perform.

### 5. Controlling
**[From the video]** Steps:
1. **Set standards** (for the goal).
2. **Measure actual performance**.
3. **Compare** performance with the standards.
4. **Take corrective action** if performance does not match. The instructor's examples: remove the employee, or motivate and train them so they improve.

### Controlling process (flow)
```
Set Standards -> Measure Performance -> Compare with Standards -> Take Corrective Action
```

### POSDCORB (Luther Gulick)
**[From the video]** The subtitles give: **P**lanning, **O**rganising, **S**taffing, **D**irecting, **Co**-ordinating, **R**eporting, **B**udgeting. This adds **coordinating, reporting and budgeting**, and has no separate "controlling" in the list.

| Letter | Function |
|---|---|
| P | Planning |
| O | Organizing |
| S | Staffing |
| D | Directing |
| CO | Co-ordinating |
| R | Reporting |
| B | Budgeting |

**[unclear in subtitles]** The later passage about "planning, organizing... controlling, with reporting and budgeting as add-ons" is garbled; it appears to say reporting and budgeting are additional to the five core functions. Verify.

### Exam-writing version
> The five functions of management are planning, organising, staffing, directing and controlling. Planning sets objectives and policies; organising groups activities and assigns duties; staffing recruits, selects and trains personnel; directing leads, motivates and supervises; and controlling sets standards, measures performance, compares it with standards and takes corrective action. Luther Gulick's POSDCORB adds co-ordinating, reporting and budgeting.

**Common confusion:** Planning *decides* the standard; controlling *checks* against it. Staffing is about *people for jobs*; directing is about *guiding people at work*.

## 2.9 Management: Science, Art, or Both?

### A. Management as a Science

**[From the video]**
- **Science** is a systematic body of knowledge, based on **hypotheses**, **tested** through experiments, giving **principles** that explain **cause-and-effect relationships**, and is **universally applicable**.
- **Management is similar to science** because it too has principles that explain cause-and-effect relationships between two or more factors, and its principles are **universally applicable**.

**But it is not a pure science.** **[From the video]** Management is a **social science**. Its principles are **not exact**; they **differ from situation to situation** because management deals with **human beings** and their behaviour, which vary. Because situations vary, the results are not the same as in pure science.

**[Additional clarification]** Management is therefore called an *inexact* or *soft* science (like economics or psychology).

### B. Management as an Art

**[From the video]**
- **Art** is the **application of knowledge, concepts, principles and techniques** in a personal and creative way.
- Management teaches you to **use** the concepts, principles and techniques.
- **Every manager applies personal skills** to deal with problems, using the principles to solve them in their own way.

### Conclusion
**[From the video]** Management is **both art and science**: the manager uses scientific principles (science) and applies them with personal skill and creativity (art).

### Comparison table
| Basis | Management as Science | Management as Art |
|---|---|---|
| Knowledge | Systematic body of principles | Application of that knowledge |
| Principles | Tested; cause-effect relationships | Used with personal skill and creativity |
| Applicability | Universal, but not exact (varies with situation) | Personal; varies from manager to manager |
| Nature | Social (inexact) science | Practical application |

### Exam-writing version
> Management is similar to science because it has a body of knowledge with principles based on cause-and-effect relationships that are universally applicable. But it is a social science, not a pure science, because human behaviour varies and principles must be adapted to situations. It is also an art because every manager applies the concepts, principles and techniques with personal skill and creativity. Hence, management is both an art and a science.

# E. Formulas, Derivations and Algorithms

There are **no numerical formulas** in this part of the lecture. The only step-by-step procedure is the **controlling process** and the **cyclic management process**.

### E1. Controlling process (algorithm)
1. Set **standards** of performance (based on goals).
2. **Measure** actual performance.
3. **Compare** actual performance with standards.
4. If performance falls short, take **corrective action**: motivate, train, or (as the instructor said) remove the person.

### E2. Management as a continuous cycle
```
Planning -> Organizing -> Staffing -> Directing -> Controlling
    ^                                                    |
    └────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
              (repeated continuously)
```

**Worked example [Additional clarification]:**
A company targets 10,000 units/month (**planning**), divides work into production, packing and dispatch teams (**organizing**), hires and trains operators (**staffing**), motivates and supervises them (**directing**), and compares weekly output with the 2,500 units/week standard, retraining a lagging team (**controlling**). The cycle repeats every month.

### Model answers (short)

**Q1. Traditional vs modern definition**
> The traditional definition (Mary Parker Follett) describes management as "the art of getting things done through others". It focuses on getting work done by others, even forcefully. The modern definition (Harold Koontz) describes management as the process of designing and maintaining an environment where individuals working in groups efficiently accomplish selected aims. It stresses creating a supportive environment and giving people facilities to perform. Hence the shift is from "getting work done" to "enabling people to perform".

**Q2. Effectiveness vs efficiency**
> Effectiveness means completing the task on time and achieving the objective. Efficiency means optimal utilisation of resources, i.e. maximum output with minimum resources. An organisation needs both: it should do the task on time and do it at low cost.

**Q3. Functions of top-level management**
> Top management determines objectives; makes plans and policies; organises activities; assembles resources; appraises overall performance; and maintains liaison with the outside world.

**Q4. Katz's skills**
> (1) Technical skill: proficiency in specific processes and techniques. (2) Human skill: ability to interact and work effectively with people. (3) Conceptual skill: ability to form ideas, analyse and solve problems. Technical skill is most needed at the lower level; conceptual skill at the top and middle levels; human skill at all levels.

**Q5. Characteristics of management** (any four)
> Universal (applicable everywhere); a continuous process (the cycle of functions repeats); both art and science; a group activity (not the work of one person); creative; a profession; an economic and social process.

**Q6. Social science**
> Management has principles like a science, but because it deals with human beings whose behaviour varies, its principles are not exact and differ from situation to situation. Hence it is a social science, not a pure science.

**Q7. Informational roles**
> The manager acts as a **monitor** (collecting information), **disseminator** (passing information downward) and **spokesperson** (speaking for the organisation).

**Q8. Controlling process**
> Set standards → measure performance → compare with standards → take corrective action (such as training, motivating or removing a poor performer).

### Model answers (long)

**Q1. Define management and explain its functions**
> **Introduction:** According to Mary Parker Follett, management is the art of getting things done through others. According to Harold Koontz, it is the process of creating and maintaining an environment in which individuals working in groups efficiently and effectively achieve group goals. Peter Drucker calls it a multipurpose organ that manages business, managers, and workers and work.
>
> **Functions:**
> 1. **Planning:** setting objectives, forecasting, budgeting, and framing policies and programs.
> 2. **Organizing:** identifying and grouping activities, assigning duties and developing relationships.
> 3. **Staffing:** recruitment, selection, training and development of personnel.
> 4. **Directing:** leading, communicating, motivating and supervising.
> 5. **Controlling:** setting standards, measuring performance, comparing, and taking corrective action.
>
> Gulick's POSDCORB adds co-ordinating, reporting and budgeting.
>
> **Conclusion:** These functions form a continuous cycle that helps an organisation be effective and efficient.

**Q2. Levels of management**
> **Introduction:** Management is divided into three levels: top, middle and lower.
> 1. **Top level** (Board of Directors, CEO, Chairman): determines objectives, makes plans and policies, organises activities, assembles resources, appraises overall performance and liaises with the outside world.
> 2. **Middle level** (production, purchase, marketing managers): interprets policies for lower levels, organises departmental activities, recruits, motivates, controls and instructs employees.
> 3. **Lower level** (supervisors, section officers, superintendents): represents workers' grievances, creates good and safe working conditions, helps in recruitment, passes on workers' suggestions, maintains quality standards and boosts morale.
>
> *(Include the pyramid diagram from section F1.)*
> **Conclusion:** The levels together ensure that plans formulated at the top are converted into actions at the lower level.

**Q3. Managerial skills**
> **Introduction:** Robert Katz identified three skills: technical, human and conceptual.
> 1. **Technical:** knowledge of specific processes and techniques; most needed at the lower level.
> 2. **Human:** ability to work with people; needed at all levels.
> 3. **Conceptual:** ability to form ideas, analyse and solve problems; most needed at top and middle levels.
>
> *(Include the skills-by-level table from section 2.6.)*
> **Conclusion:** A successful manager must combine all three skills in proportion to the level at which they work.

**Q4. Roles of a manager**
> **Introduction:** Henry Mintzberg grouped managerial roles into three categories.
> 1. **Interpersonal roles:** figurehead (represents the organisation), leader, liaison (maintains relationships).
> 2. **Informational roles:** monitor, disseminator (passes information down), spokesperson.
> 3. **Decisional roles:** entrepreneur (decides what, how much and how to produce), disturbance handler, resource allocator, negotiator.
>
> *(Include the diagram from section 2.7.)*
> **Conclusion:** These roles show that a manager's job is much broader than just supervising people.

## Key Takeaways
- Management = getting things done through others (traditional) / creating an environment for group performance (modern).

- Five functions: **P-O-S-D-C**. POSDCORB adds **Co-ordinating, Reporting, Budgeting**.

## Important Definitions to Memorize
Drucker's, Follett's and Koontz's definitions of management; effectiveness; efficiency; profession; art; science.

- **Controlling steps:** Standards → Measure → Compare → Correct.
- **Skills by level:** Top/Middle = conceptual (+ human); Lower = technical (+ human).
- **Mintzberg's ten roles:** Figurehead, Leader, Liaison; Monitor, Disseminator, Spokesperson; Entrepreneur, Disturbance handler, Resource allocator, Negotiator.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid
1. Confusing **effectiveness** (on time/goal achieved) with **efficiency** (resource use).
2. Treating management as a *pure* science. It is a **social** science.
3. Saying management is a *fully fledged* profession without mentioning the lack of regulated entry.
4. Mixing up levels and giving "CEO" as a middle-level example.
5. Forgetting that **human skill is needed at all levels**.
6. Listing Mintzberg's roles without grouping them under the three categories.
