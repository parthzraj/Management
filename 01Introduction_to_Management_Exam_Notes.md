## 1. Topic Overview

### Introduction

Management is required whenever people work together to achieve a common goal. It helps an organisation plan its work, arrange resources, guide employees, and check whether the work is progressing as intended. Management is relevant to businesses such as factories and hotels, as well as hospitals, educational institutions, households, and other organised activities.

### Learning objectives

After studying these notes, you should be able to:

- Define management in clear, exam-ready language.
- Explain management as a process of related activities.
- Distinguish **effectiveness** from **efficiency**.
- Describe the main characteristics of management discussed in the video.
- Explain the three dimensions of management: management of work, people, and operations.
- Discuss organisational, social, and individual objectives of management.
- Apply the concepts to a practical example, such as a furniture factory.

### Major concepts covered in the provided video

- Management as a process directed towards goals.
- Effectiveness: achieving the intended goal within the required time.
- Efficiency: using resources economically to obtain the desired output.
- Management as goal-oriented, universal, multidimensional, continuous, group-based, dynamic, and intangible.

## 2. Meaning and Definition of Management

### 2.1 Exam-ready definition

**Management is the process of planning, organising, staffing, directing, and controlling resources and activities to achieve organisational objectives effectively and efficiently.**

The video describes management as a process involving a series of interrelated steps or functions through which a goal is achieved. The five functions named in the subtitles are planning, organising, staffing, directing, and controlling.

### 2.2 In simple words

Management means deciding what needs to be done, arranging the people and resources required, guiding the work, and checking that the desired result is achieved without wasting time or resources.

For example, a student preparing for university examinations manages their time by deciding which subjects to study, allocating hours to each subject, following a timetable, and checking progress through revision and practice tests.

### 2.3 Management is a process

A **process** is a series of connected steps or activities carried out in an organised way to achieve a result.

Management is a process because its functions are related to one another. They are not isolated activities.

```text
Planning
   ↓
Organising
   ↓
Staffing
   ↓
Directing
   ↓
Controlling
   ↺ Feedback and adjustments to future plans
```

**How to understand the diagram:** Planning sets the goal and decides how to achieve it. Organising arranges tasks and resources. Staffing ensures that suitable people are available. Directing guides and motivates employees. Controlling compares actual performance with the plan and helps correct deviations.

> **Additional clarification:** In practice, these functions may overlap and managers may return to an earlier function when conditions change. The diagram is a useful learning sequence, not a claim that every management activity always happens in a perfectly linear order.

### 2.4 Example: managing a furniture factory

Suppose a furniture factory receives an order to manufacture and deliver 20 cupboards by 1 March.

1. **Planning:** Determine the number of cupboards, materials, workers, budget, and completion schedule.
2. **Organising:** Assign tasks, arrange machines and raw materials, and coordinate departments.
3. **Staffing:** Ensure that enough carpenters and other workers with the required skills are available.
4. **Directing:** Explain responsibilities, guide workers, and motivate them.
5. **Controlling:** Check quality, production progress, costs, and whether delivery will occur by the deadline.

The factory needs management because the order requires several connected activities and resources to be coordinated.

### 2.5 Exam-writing answer

Management is a process through which organisational goals are achieved by coordinating people and other resources. It consists of interrelated functions such as planning, organising, staffing, directing, and controlling. These functions help an organisation arrange its work systematically, use resources properly, and achieve its objectives. For example, a furniture factory uses management to plan production, arrange materials and workers, guide production, and ensure that the finished furniture is delivered on time.

## 3. Effectiveness and Efficiency

The video repeatedly emphasises that good management requires both effectiveness and efficiency.

### 3.1 Effectiveness

**Definition:** Effectiveness is the extent to which intended objectives are achieved. In the examples in the video, it also involves completing the required work within the specified time.

In simple words, effectiveness means **doing the right work and achieving the intended result**.

**Example:** If a factory is required to deliver 20 cupboards by 1 March and delivers the correct order by that date, it has achieved the objective effectively.

Key points:
- Focuses on achieving the goal.
- Considers whether the required output or result was obtained.
- Deadlines matter when they are part of the objective.
- A task completed late may fail to meet the intended objective, even if the product is eventually finished.

### 3.2 Efficiency

**Definition:** Efficiency is the ability to achieve the desired output with the least reasonable use of resources, such as time, money, materials, labour, and equipment.

In simple words, efficiency means **doing the work with minimum waste**.

**Example:** Two factories may both deliver 20 cupboards on time. If one factory uses less material and avoids unnecessary costs while maintaining the required quality, it is more efficient.

The video explains efficiency through the idea of obtaining more output or benefit from fewer inputs. It also mentions comparing costs with benefits.

A simplified way to express this idea is:

\[
\text{Efficiency} \approx \frac{\text{Useful output}}{\text{Resources used}}
\]

This is a conceptual relationship, not a universal formula for every management situation. In real organisations, efficiency may be measured using specific indicators such as units produced per labour-hour or cost per unit.

### 3.3 Input and output

- **Input:** Resources used to perform work, such as money, labour, raw materials, energy, and time.
- **Output:** The result produced, such as cupboards manufactured, customers served, or services delivered.

Example: If a workshop uses wood, labour, electricity, and machine time to produce tables, these resources are inputs and the completed tables are outputs.

### 3.4 Cost-benefit analysis

**Cost-benefit analysis** means comparing the costs of an activity with the benefits expected or obtained from it.

For example, a factory should consider whether buying a new machine will improve production enough to justify its purchase, maintenance, training, and operating costs. Buying expensive machinery that workers cannot use properly may increase costs without providing a matching benefit.

### 3.5 Why both effectiveness and efficiency are necessary

| Situation | Effective? | Efficient? | Explanation |
|---|---|---|---|
| The correct order is delivered on time using resources carefully | Yes | Yes | The goal is achieved without unnecessary waste |
| The correct order is delivered on time, but costs are unnecessarily high | Yes | No or low efficiency | The goal is achieved, but resources are wasted |
| The order is produced cheaply but delivered after the deadline | No, if the deadline is part of the objective | Possibly efficient in resource use | Costs may be low, but the required objective was missed |
| The order is late and costs much more than planned | No | No | The goal and resource-use expectations are both missed |

### 3.6 Common confusion

Do not write that effectiveness and efficiency mean the same thing.

- **Effectiveness asks:** Did we achieve the intended goal?
- **Efficiency asks:** How economically did we use our resources to achieve it?

A manager should try to achieve goals effectively while using resources efficiently.

### 3.7 Exam-writing answer: effectiveness versus efficiency

Effectiveness refers to the achievement of planned objectives, while efficiency refers to achieving the desired output with minimum waste of resources. Effectiveness focuses mainly on the result, whereas efficiency focuses on the relationship between output and inputs. For example, a factory that delivers an order on time is effective. If it also controls material, labour, and production costs without reducing quality, it is efficient. Good management requires both effectiveness and efficiency.

## 4. Characteristics of Management

The subtitles discuss several characteristics of management. The following explanations preserve the concepts that can be recovered from the transcription. Some textbook labels are standardised for exam use; they should not be mistaken for a verbatim list from the instructor.

### 4.1 Management is goal-oriented

**Definition:** Management is goal-oriented because its activities are performed to achieve specific objectives.

Explanation:
- An organisation sets targets.
- Managers coordinate people and resources to reach those targets.
- Decisions and activities should contribute to the stated objectives.

**Example:** A furniture factory coordinates workers and machines to complete a customer's cupboard order before the agreed date.

**Exam point:** Management is not a random collection of activities; it is directed towards defined goals.

### 4.2 Management is universal

**Definition:** Management is universal because it is needed in different types and sizes of organisations and in many areas of life.

The video gives examples such as factories, hospitals, hotels, colleges, homes, and other organisations.

Explanation:
- A factory manages production and delivery.
- A hospital coordinates appointments, staff, and patient services.
- A college manages classes, staff, rooms, and examinations.
- A household manages time, money, and responsibilities.

The specific activities differ, but the need to coordinate work and resources is common.

### 4.3 Management is multidimensional

**Definition:** Management is multidimensional because it involves managing work, people, and operations.

These three dimensions are explained in Section 5.

- **Management of work:** Defining and completing the tasks needed to achieve goals.
- **Management of people:** Coordinating employees while considering their abilities and needs.
- **Management of operations:** Managing the processes through which inputs are converted into outputs.

### 4.4 Management is a continuous process

**Definition:** Management is continuous because it continues as long as an organisation operates and its objectives and conditions require attention.

Explanation:
- After one order is completed, a factory may receive another.
- Managers may need to plan new work, arrange materials, recruit workers, guide employees, and monitor production again.
- Managers also respond to feedback and changing circumstances.

**Example:** Completing one furniture order does not end the need for management. The factory must prepare for its next order and continue coordinating its activities.

### 4.5 Management is a group activity

**Definition:** Management is a group activity because organisational goals are generally achieved through the coordinated efforts of multiple people and departments.

The video describes how a production team may coordinate with purchasing and human-resources teams to fulfil an order.

**Example:**
1. The sales team communicates the customer's order.
2. The production team determines the materials and labour required.
3. The purchasing team arranges raw materials if stocks are insufficient.
4. The human-resources team helps provide the required workers.
5. The production team manufactures the product and coordinates delivery.

The departments must cooperate for the common organisational goal to be achieved.

### 4.6 Management is dynamic

**Definition:** Management is dynamic because it adapts to changes in the business environment and changing requirements.

The video compares earlier classroom teaching methods with the increased use of online teaching during the COVID-19 period. The intended point is that organisations and managers may need to change their methods when circumstances change.

Explanation:
- Technology changes.
- Customer expectations change.
- Competition and market conditions change.
- Laws and working conditions may change.
- Managers must adjust plans and methods where necessary.

**Example:** An educational organisation may adopt online classes when physical classes are disrupted.

### 4.7 Management is an intangible force

**Definition:** Management is intangible because it cannot usually be seen as a physical object, but its effects can be observed in how an organisation functions.

The video explains that a visitor may notice orderly reception, shorter waiting times, clear information, coordinated staff, and systematic service. These visible outcomes can reflect effective management, even though management itself is not a physical object.

**Example:** In a well-managed factory, employees understand their tasks, materials are available when needed, and production proceeds in an organised manner.

**Important distinction:** Management itself is intangible, but its results—such as products, reports, service quality, and production figures—can be observed or measured.

### 4.8 Human resources are important

The subtitles stress that machines and technology do not manage themselves: people design, operate, coordinate, and maintain them. Employees are therefore a major organisational resource.

Management of people involves:
- Assigning responsibilities.
- Coordinating individual efforts.
- Providing suitable working conditions.
- Considering employees' needs and capabilities.
- Motivating employees and recognising their contributions.

> **Additional clarification:** Human-resource management is broader than simply getting people to work harder. Sustainable performance also depends on skills, safety, fair treatment, suitable tools, and clear expectations.

| Goal-oriented | Directed towards defined objectives | Completing a customer order |
| Universal | Needed across many types of organisations | Hospital, college, factory, household |
| Multidimensional | Involves work, people, and operations | Coordinating production tasks and workers |
| Continuous | Ongoing while organisational work continues | Managing one order after another |

### 4.10 Exam-writing answer: characteristics of management

Management has several important characteristics. It is **goal-oriented**, because its activities aim to achieve specific objectives. It is **universal**, because it is required in factories, hospitals, colleges, hotels, and other organisations. It is **multidimensional**, involving the management of work, people, and operations. It is a **continuous process**, as management activities continue while organisational work continues. It is a **group activity**, because different people and departments must cooperate. It is **dynamic**, because managers adapt to changes in technology and the business environment. Finally, management is an **intangible force**: it cannot normally be touched or seen directly, but its effects can be observed in the orderly functioning of an organisation.

## 5. Three Dimensions of Management

The video explains management through three dimensions: management of work, management of people, and management of operations.

### 5.1 Management of work

**Definition:** Management of work means identifying, organising, and completing the tasks required to achieve organisational objectives.

It includes clarifying:
- What work must be done.
- What result is expected.
- How the work should be performed.
- When it must be completed.
- How progress and quality will be checked.

**Example:** In a furniture factory, the work may be to manufacture a specified number of cupboards to a required design and deadline.

### 5.2 Management of people

**Definition:** Management of people means coordinating and guiding employees so that their efforts contribute to organisational goals while their relevant needs and capabilities are considered.

The video notes that different individuals have different needs. Managers therefore need to coordinate employees and consider matters such as pay, incentives, recognition, motivation, and working conditions.

**Example:** A factory manager allocates work to carpenters based on their skills, explains production targets, ensures they have the required materials, and recognises good performance.

### 5.3 Management of operations

**Definition:** Management of operations means coordinating the processes and activities through which an organisation uses resources to produce goods or deliver services.

In a factory, operations may include receiving raw materials, preparing components, assembling products, inspecting quality, storing finished goods, and arranging delivery.

**Example:** The factory must coordinate the flow of wood, machine time, labour, assembly, quality checks, and dispatch so that the final cupboards are completed as required.

### 5.4 Diagram

```text
                    MANAGEMENT
                         |
        +----------------+----------------+
        |                |                |
 Management of     Management of    Management of
     work              people          operations
        |                |                |
 Tasks and goals    Employees and     Processes that turn
                    coordination       inputs into outputs
```

### 5.5 Why the three dimensions must work together

A factory cannot succeed by managing only one dimension:
- If tasks are unclear, employees may work on the wrong priorities.
- If people are not properly coordinated, work may be delayed or performed poorly.
- If operations are poorly organised, materials and machines may be wasted.

The three dimensions support one another and help the organisation achieve its objectives.

### 5.6 Exam-writing answer

Management is multidimensional because it involves three major dimensions. First, **management of work** means identifying and completing the tasks required to achieve organisational goals. Second, **management of people** means coordinating employees, assigning responsibilities, and considering their skills and needs. Third, **management of operations** means managing the processes through which resources are converted into goods or services. In a furniture factory, these dimensions involve defining the order, coordinating carpenters and other employees, and managing material supply, production, quality checking, and delivery.

## 6. Objectives of Management

The video groups management objectives into three broad categories: organisational objectives, social objectives, and individual objectives.

### 6.1 Organisational objectives

Organisational objectives concern the continued operation and development of the organisation. The video identifies three objectives: **survival, profit, and growth**.

#### A. Survival

**Definition:** Survival is the objective of keeping the organisation operating by earning enough to meet its costs and continue its activities.

In the video’s example, a new factory has monthly expenses such as rent, electricity, and employee salaries. If these expenses total ₹1,00,000 per month, the factory needs sufficient income to cover them and continue operating.

Key points:
- Especially important for a new business.
- The organisation must cover its costs and maintain its operations.
- Without sufficient resources or income, continued operation becomes difficult.

**Example:** A new furniture factory first focuses on generating enough revenue to pay rent, salaries, electricity, and other essential costs.

#### B. Profit

**Definition:** Profit is the financial surplus remaining when total costs are subtracted from total revenue.

\[
\text{Profit} = \text{Total Revenue} - \text{Total Cost}
\]

**Example:** If a factory earns ₹2,00,000 in revenue and incurs total costs of ₹1,60,000 during a period, its profit is ₹40,000.

Profit can help a business reward employees, invest in equipment, build reserves, and support future growth. The video presents profit as an important objective of a business organisation.

#### C. Growth

**Definition:** Growth means expanding the organisation's size, operations, sales, market reach, or range of products and services over time.

The video gives the example of a furniture factory that initially makes cupboards and may later add tables, chairs, beds, and other products.

Possible signs of growth include:
- Higher sales volume.
- More customers or a larger market share.
- A wider product range.
- Increased production capacity.
- More employees or additional locations.

**Important clarification:** Growth should be managed sustainably. Expansion that creates costs the organisation cannot support may threaten survival or profitability.

### 6.2 Social objectives

**Definition:** Social objectives are the responsibilities of an organisation towards society and the communities affected by its activities.

The video identifies the following social objectives:

1. **Environment-friendly production:** Use production methods that avoid unnecessary pollution and environmental damage.
2. **Employment opportunities:** Create jobs as the organisation develops.
3. **Good-quality products:** Provide products that meet appropriate quality expectations.
4. **Basic amenities and responsible conduct:** The subtitles mention providing basic amenities and following the law, though the exact wording around these points is partly unclear.

**Example:** A furniture factory can reduce avoidable waste, follow applicable safety and environmental rules, provide employment, and sell furniture that meets its stated quality standards.

### 6.3 Individual objectives

**Definition:** Individual objectives are the personal and work-related goals of employees, which management should consider while pursuing organisational objectives.

The video mentions employee needs and expectations such as:
- Salary and financial benefits.
- Incentives and bonuses.
- Recognition for work.
- Motivation.
- Suitable working conditions and leave.

The central idea is that employees are individuals with their own needs. If those needs are consistently ignored, motivation and interest in work may decline, which can affect organisational performance.

**Example:** A manager who pays employees correctly, provides safe working conditions, recognises good performance, and communicates expectations clearly is more likely to support employee commitment.

### 6.4 Relationship between the three types of objectives

Management must coordinate organisational, social, and individual objectives rather than considering only one.

```text
                 EFFECTIVE MANAGEMENT
                          |
        +-----------------+-----------------+
        |                 |                 |
 Organisational         Social           Individual
   objectives         objectives         objectives
        |                 |                 |
 Survival, profit,   Society, quality,   Employee needs,
 and growth          environment, jobs   rewards, recognition
        +-----------------+-----------------+
                          |
               Sustainable performance
```

**Example:** A furniture factory may aim to earn profit and grow, while also reducing waste, producing safe and good-quality furniture, complying with the law, and treating employees fairly. These objectives can support one another over time.

### 6.5 Exam-writing answer: objectives of management

The objectives of management can be classified into three categories. **Organisational objectives** include survival, profit, and growth. Survival means keeping the organisation operating by covering its essential costs. Profit is the surplus of revenue over total costs. Growth means expanding sales, operations, market reach, or product range. **Social objectives** include environmentally responsible production, creating employment, providing good-quality products, and complying with relevant laws. **Individual objectives** involve meeting employees' reasonable needs through salary, incentives, recognition, motivation, and suitable working conditions. Management attempts to coordinate these objectives so that the organisation performs effectively and responsibly.

Profit = Total Revenue − Total Cost  
= ₹2,50,000 − ₹2,05,000  
= **₹45,000**

**Q2. Two factories both deliver 100 chairs on time. Factory A uses more material and incurs avoidable costs; Factory B meets the same quality requirement with less waste. Which is more efficient, and why?**  
**Model answer:** Factory B is more efficient because it achieves the required output with less waste of resources. Both may be effective if both meet the target and deadline, but Factory B uses resources more economically.

**Q3. A new factory cannot cover rent, salaries, electricity, and other essential expenses. Which organisational objective is at risk?**  
**Model answer:** Survival is at risk because the factory is not generating or securing enough resources to meet its essential costs and continue operating.
