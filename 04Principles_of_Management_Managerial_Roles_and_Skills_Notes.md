## 2. Managerial Roles: Meaning

**Definition:** A managerial role is a pattern of behaviour or a set of activities that a manager performs as part of their work in an organisation.

In simple terms, managerial roles describe **what managers do** while managing people, information, decisions, and organisational activities.

Henry Mintzberg grouped managerial roles into three broad categories:

1. **Interpersonal roles** — working with people and maintaining relationships.
2. **Informational roles** — collecting, sharing, and communicating information.
3. **Decisional roles** — making choices, allocating resources, handling problems, and negotiating.

Mintzberg identified **10 roles** in total: 3 interpersonal, 3 informational, and 4 decisional.

## 3. Mintzberg's Managerial Roles

### 3.1 Interpersonal roles

Interpersonal roles arise from a manager's formal authority and relationships with other people. The three roles are **figurehead, leader, and liaison**.

#### A. Figurehead

**Meaning:** The manager performs ceremonial and symbolic duties and represents the organisation.

**Main activities:**

- Attending official functions and public events.
- Greeting and hosting visitors or clients.
- Representing the organisation at formal occasions.
- Performing duties associated with the manager's official position.

**Example:** A company's director welcomes visiting business partners and represents the company at an award ceremony.

**Exam keyword:** *Ceremonial and symbolic representation.*

#### B. Leader

**Meaning:** The manager guides, motivates, and directs employees to achieve organisational objectives.

**Main activities:**

- Setting goals for team members.
- Providing guidance and direction.
- Motivating and supporting employees.
- Helping employees perform their work and develop their capabilities.

**Example:** A department manager explains the team's targets, motivates staff, and provides guidance when employees face difficulties.

**Exam keyword:** *Guiding and motivating subordinates.*

#### C. Liaison

**Meaning:** The manager acts as a link or bridge between people, departments, and external stakeholders.

**Main activities:**

- Building relationships across departments.
- Facilitating communication and cooperation.
- Connecting the organisation with relevant external contacts.
- Helping different groups exchange information.

**Example from the lecture:** The production department needs to know how much the sales department sold last month before deciding how much to produce this month. A manager helps the two departments communicate and coordinate their plans.

**Exam keyword:** *Bridge between departments and contacts.*

### 3.2 Informational roles

Informational roles concern collecting, processing, and communicating information. The three roles are **monitor, disseminator, and spokesperson**.

#### A. Monitor

**Meaning:** The manager gathers and observes relevant information about what is happening inside and outside the organisation.

**Main activities:**

- Monitoring organisational performance.
- Gathering reports, updates, and feedback.
- Observing changes in the business environment.
- Identifying issues that may affect organisational goals.

**Example:** A sales manager reviews sales reports, customer feedback, and competitor activity to understand business performance.

**Exam keyword:** *Collecting and monitoring information.*

#### B. Disseminator

**Meaning:** The manager passes relevant information to people within the organisation.

**Main activities:**

- Sharing important updates, plans, and changes with employees.
- Communicating information from senior management to the appropriate teams.
- Ensuring that employees receive information needed for their work.

**Example:** Top management announces a revised company policy. Departmental managers explain the policy to their teams and clarify how it affects their work.

**Exam keyword:** *Sharing information internally.*

#### C. Spokesperson

**Meaning:** The manager communicates information about the organisation to people outside it or represents the organisation publicly.

**Main activities:**

- Explaining organisational plans, policies, and goals to external audiences.
- Communicating with the media, clients, investors, or other relevant groups.
- Representing the organisation at public events and official discussions.

**Example:** A company executive explains the company's new sustainability policy to the press.

**Exam keyword:** *Communicating externally on behalf of the organisation.*

### 3.3 Decisional roles

Decisional roles involve making choices and taking action. Mintzberg's four roles are **entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, and negotiator**.

#### A. Entrepreneur

**Meaning:** The manager initiates or encourages change, innovation, and improvement.

**Main activities:**

- Identifying opportunities for improvement.
- Developing or encouraging new ideas.
- Initiating projects and changes.
- Implementing innovations that can improve organisational performance.

**Example:** A manager introduces a new inventory system to reduce delays and stock wastage.

**Exam keyword:** *Initiating change and innovation.*

**Important distinction:** In Mintzberg's model, *entrepreneur* is a managerial role. It does not mean the manager must own or start a separate business.

#### B. Disturbance handler

**Meaning:** The manager responds to unexpected problems, crises, disputes, or conflicts and works to resolve them.

**Main activities:**

- Handling internal disagreements.
- Responding to unexpected operational problems.
- Resolving conflicts between teams or with external stakeholders.
- Taking corrective action when normal operations are disrupted.

**Example:** Two departments disagree about responsibility for a delayed order. The manager investigates the issue and coordinates a solution.

**Exam keyword:** *Resolving conflicts and unexpected disruptions.*

**Stakeholders vs shareholders:**

- **Stakeholders** are people or groups affected by, or connected with, an organisation. Examples include employees, customers, suppliers, lenders, government bodies, and the local community.
- **Shareholders** own shares in a company and therefore hold an ownership interest.

Shareholders are one type of stakeholder, but not all stakeholders are shareholders.

#### C. Resource allocator

**Meaning:** The manager decides how organisational resources should be distributed and used.

**Resources may include:**

- Time.
- Money and budgets.
- Employees and other manpower.
- Raw materials.
- Equipment and other physical resources.

**Main activities:**

- Setting priorities for the use of resources.
- Assigning budgets and staff to activities.
- Ensuring resources are available where and when needed.
- Balancing competing demands within available time and budget.

**Example:** A project manager allocates employees, equipment, and budget across several projects according to their importance and deadlines.

**Exam keyword:** *Allocating resources effectively.*

#### D. Negotiator

**Meaning:** The manager negotiates with other parties to reach an acceptable agreement.

**Main activities:**

- Discussing terms with suppliers, clients, or partners.
- Negotiating prices, deadlines, or responsibilities.
- Resolving differences through discussion.
- Seeking agreements that are workable and beneficial to the parties involved.

**Example from the lecture:** A company and a raw-material supplier disagree about price. The manager negotiates terms that both sides can accept.

**Exam keyword:** *Reaching agreements through negotiation.*

## 4. Managerial Skills: Meaning

**Definition:** Managerial skills are the abilities and competencies that managers need to perform their responsibilities and achieve organisational objectives effectively.

In simple terms, managerial skills describe **what a manager needs to be good at** in order to perform the job.

The lecture attributes the three-skill framework to **Robert L. Katz**:

1. Technical skills.
2. Human skills.
3. Conceptual skills.

The lecture associates technical skills most strongly with lower-level management, human skills with middle-level management, and conceptual skills with top-level management. These are emphases rather than absolute rules: all managers benefit from all three skills, but the relative importance changes with the role.

## 5. Katz's Three Managerial Skills

### 5.1 Technical skills

**Definition:** Technical skills are the knowledge and ability to use specific methods, processes, tools, equipment, or specialised techniques in a particular area of work.

**Most emphasised at:** Lower/operational management.

**Why they matter:** Frontline supervisors often need to understand the work being performed so they can guide employees, recognise technical problems, and help maintain output and quality.

**Example from the lecture:** If a machine in a production department stops working, a supervisor with relevant technical knowledge can identify the problem or coordinate the correct technical response.

**Other examples:**

- Understanding a production process.
- Using relevant software or equipment.
- Knowing quality-control procedures.
- Troubleshooting a technical issue in a functional area.

**Exam keyword:** *Specialised, job-related knowledge and methods.*

### 5.2 Human skills

**Definition:** Human skills are the ability to work effectively with people, communicate clearly, build relationships, understand others, and cooperate with a team.

The lecture also refers to these as **interpersonal skills**.

**Most emphasised at:** Middle-level management in the lecture.

**Why they matter:** Middle managers communicate with senior management above them and employees or supervisors below them. They must explain plans, listen to concerns, coordinate departments, motivate people, and maintain working relationships.

**Examples:**

- Explaining a new policy clearly.
- Resolving a disagreement between team members.
- Motivating employees.
- Giving constructive feedback.
- Coordinating work between departments.

**Exam keyword:** *Communication and relationships with people.*

### 5.3 Conceptual skills

**Definition:** Conceptual skills are the ability to understand the organisation as a whole, see how its different parts relate to one another, analyse complex situations, and think strategically.

**Most emphasised at:** Top-level management.

**Why they matter:** Senior managers make decisions that affect the entire organisation. They need to consider the organisation's vision, strategy, external environment, long-term goals, and the relationships between departments.

**Examples:**

- Developing a long-term business strategy.
- Understanding how a change in marketing affects production and finance.
- Analysing a complex organisational problem.
- Making informed strategic decisions.
- Setting an overall direction for the organisation.

**Exam keyword:** *Strategic thinking and understanding the organisation as a whole.*

### 5.4 Relationship between management level and skill

| Management level        | Skill emphasised in the lecture | Why it matters                                                      | Example                                    |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Top level               | Conceptual                      | Strategy, vision, complex decisions, whole-organisation perspective | Deciding to enter a new market             |
| Middle level            | Human                           | Communicating with senior managers and teams; coordinating people   | Explaining a new plan to departments       |
| Lower/operational level | Technical                       | Understanding work methods and solving job-related problems         | Helping resolve a production-process issue |

**Important qualification:** This table reflects the lecture's simplified association. In practice, technical skills are useful at every level, human skills are important at every level, and conceptual skills become especially important as responsibility for the whole organisation increases.

### 5.5 All three skills in one example

Imagine a manufacturing company is considering a new production line:

- **Top manager — conceptual skill:** Evaluates whether the investment fits the company's long-term strategy and budget.
- **Middle manager — human skill:** Coordinates production, finance, and HR teams and communicates the implementation plan.
- **Lower manager — technical skill:** Helps employees use the equipment correctly and identifies practical process problems.

This shows how the three skills complement one another.

### 6.1 Managerial roles vs managerial skills

### 6.2 Monitor vs disseminator vs spokesperson

| Role         | Main direction of information                           | Core action                      |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Monitor      | Information is gathered from relevant sources           | Collect and observe              |
| Disseminator | Information is passed to people inside the organisation | Share internally                 |
| Spokesperson | Information is communicated to external audiences       | Represent and explain externally |

### 6.3 Figurehead vs spokesperson

Both roles involve representing the organisation, but their emphasis differs:

- **Figurehead:** formal, ceremonial, and symbolic duties.
- **Spokesperson:** communicating the organisation's goals, plans, policies, or position to external audiences.

### 6.4 Disturbance handler vs negotiator

- **Disturbance handler:** responds to conflicts, crises, or unexpected disruptions and works to resolve them.
- **Negotiator:** discusses terms with another party to reach an agreement.

Negotiation can help resolve a disturbance, but the roles are not identical.

### B. Short-answer questions

**1. Explain Mintzberg's interpersonal roles.**

Mintzberg identified three interpersonal roles:

1. **Figurehead:** Performs formal, ceremonial, and symbolic duties and represents the organisation.
2. **Leader:** Guides, motivates, and directs employees towards organisational objectives.
3. **Liaison:** Maintains relationships and acts as a bridge between departments and external contacts.

**2. Explain Mintzberg's informational roles.**

The three informational roles are:

1. **Monitor:** Collects and observes information about organisational performance and the environment.
2. **Disseminator:** Shares relevant information with employees and other internal members.
3. **Spokesperson:** Communicates information about the organisation to external audiences.

**3. Explain Mintzberg's decisional roles.**

The four decisional roles are:

1. **Entrepreneur:** Initiates innovation and organisational improvement.
2. **Disturbance handler:** Responds to conflicts, crises, and unexpected problems.
3. **Resource allocator:** Distributes time, money, staff, materials, and other resources.
4. **Negotiator:** Negotiates with suppliers, clients, partners, or other parties to reach agreements.

**4. Explain technical, human, and conceptual skills.**

- **Technical skills:** Specialised knowledge and ability to perform or understand specific job-related methods and processes.
- **Human skills:** Ability to communicate, cooperate, motivate, and build effective relationships with people.
- **Conceptual skills:** Ability to understand the organisation as a whole, analyse complex situations, and think strategically.

**5. Distinguish managerial roles from managerial skills.**

Managerial roles describe the activities a manager performs, such as leading, monitoring, and negotiating. Managerial skills describe the abilities required to perform those activities, such as communication, technical knowledge, and strategic thinking. Roles answer “what does a manager do?” while skills answer “what must a manager be able to do well?”

### C. Long-answer questions

**1. Explain Mintzberg's ten managerial roles with examples.**

**Introduction:** Henry Mintzberg classified managerial work into three categories: interpersonal, informational, and decisional roles.

**Interpersonal roles:**

1. **Figurehead:** Performs ceremonial duties and represents the organisation; for example, welcoming official visitors.
2. **Leader:** Guides and motivates employees; for example, helping a team meet its targets.
3. **Liaison:** Connects departments and external contacts; for example, coordinating production and sales.

**Informational roles:**
4. **Monitor:** Collects and observes information; for example, reviewing performance reports.
5. **Disseminator:** Shares information internally; for example, explaining a new policy to employees.
6. **Spokesperson:** Communicates externally; for example, presenting the company's plans to the press.

**Decisional roles:**
7. **Entrepreneur:** Initiates change and innovation; for example, introducing a new process.
8. **Disturbance handler:** Resolves conflicts and unexpected problems; for example, settling a dispute between departments.
9. **Resource allocator:** Distributes organisational resources; for example, assigning budgets and staff to projects.
10. **Negotiator:** Negotiates agreements; for example, discussing prices and delivery terms with a supplier.

**Conclusion:** The ten roles show that managers work with people, handle information, and make decisions. These activities overlap in real organisational life.

**2. Explain Katz's three managerial skills and their importance at different management levels.**

**Introduction:** Robert L. Katz's framework identifies technical, human, and conceptual skills as important managerial capabilities.

1. **Technical skills:** Specialised knowledge of methods, tools, and processes. They are especially important for lower-level managers, who supervise daily work and may need to understand job-related problems.
2. **Human skills:** The ability to communicate, cooperate, motivate, and build relationships. The lecture emphasises these for middle-level managers because they connect senior management with frontline teams.
3. **Conceptual skills:** The ability to understand the organisation as a whole, analyse complex situations, and make strategic decisions. These are especially important for top-level managers, who set the organisation's direction.

**Conclusion:** All three skills are useful at every level, but their relative importance changes with managerial responsibilities. Technical skills are most directly associated with specific operations, human skills support work with people, and conceptual skills support organisation-wide thinking.

**3. Discuss the relationship between Mintzberg's roles and Katz's skills.**

Mintzberg's framework describes what managers do, while Katz's framework describes the skills they need to do it effectively. For example, the leader role requires human skills to motivate and guide employees; the monitor role benefits from analytical ability and job knowledge; and the entrepreneur role often requires conceptual thinking to identify opportunities and plan changes. A manager's work therefore requires a combination of roles and skills rather than a single ability.

### Common exam mistakes to avoid

- Do not confuse **liaison** (connecting people and groups) with **spokesperson** (communicating externally).
- Do not confuse **monitor** (gathering information) with **disseminator** (sharing information internally).
- Do not confuse **disturbance handler** (responding to a crisis or conflict) with **negotiator** (reaching an agreement).
- Do not write that only one management level needs one particular skill. The lecture gives the strongest association, but all three skills can be useful at every level.
- Remember the names accurately: **Henry Mintzberg** for managerial roles and **Robert L. Katz** for the three-skill framework.
