# Evolution of Management — Exam-Oriented Notes

## 1. Introduction to the Evolution of Management

### Definition of Management

Management is the process of planning, organizing, staffing, directing, and controlling the available resources of an organization to achieve its objectives efficiently and effectively.

- **Efficiency:** Completing work with minimum waste of time, money, and resources.
- **Effectiveness:** Achieving the desired objectives or goals.

**Example:** A factory manager who produces 1,000 units of good-quality products while minimizing material wastage is both efficient and effective.

### Meaning of Evolution of Management

The evolution of management refers to the gradual development of management theories, principles, and practices over time. As organizations became larger and more complex, managers developed different methods to improve productivity, coordinate employees, and achieve organizational objectives.

Initially, management focused mainly on supervision, discipline, and production. Later, it began emphasizing employee motivation, organizational relationships, environmental changes, and systematic decision-making.

### Need for the Evolution of Management

The evolution of management was necessary because of the following reasons:

1. **Industrial Revolution:** The growth of factories created a need for systematic planning, supervision, and coordination.
2. **Increase in organizational size:** Large organizations required formal structures and clearly defined responsibilities.
3. **Need for higher productivity:** Businesses searched for methods to reduce costs and improve output.
4. **Changing employee expectations:** Workers increasingly demanded fair wages, better working conditions, and recognition.
5. **Technological development:** New technologies required changes in organizational processes and managerial practices.
6. **Global competition:** Organizations needed flexible and effective management strategies.
7. **Changing business environment:** Managers needed to consider external factors such as customers, competitors, government policies, and economic conditions.

### Major Stages in the Evolution of Management

The major approaches to management developed over time as follows:

1. Classical Approach
   - Scientific Management
   - Administrative Management
   - Bureaucratic Management
2. Neo-Classical Approach
   - Human Relations Approach
   - Behavioural Approach
3. Modern Approach
   - Systems Approach
   - Contingency Approach

**Note:** The approaches overlap historically. They did not replace one another completely; modern organizations often use ideas from several approaches together.

# 2. Scientific Management Approach

## 2.1 Meaning and Definition

Scientific Management is a management approach that applies scientific methods, observation, measurement, and experimentation to determine the most efficient way of performing a job.

It aims to improve productivity by replacing traditional methods of working with scientifically developed procedures.

The approach is mainly associated with **Frederick Winslow Taylor**, who is known as the *Father of Scientific Management*.

### Historical Background

Scientific Management developed during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, when industrial organizations were trying to increase production and reduce waste.

Taylor observed that workers often performed the same tasks using different methods. He believed that productivity could be improved by scientifically identifying the best method for each job.

## 2.2 Principles of Scientific Management

Taylor proposed four major principles.

### 1. Science, Not Rule of Thumb

Management should replace traditional guesswork, personal opinions, and trial-and-error methods with scientific analysis.

Every task should be studied to determine the most efficient method of performing it.

**Example:** Instead of allowing each worker to decide how to assemble a product, a company studies different methods and standardizes the most efficient procedure.

### 2. Harmony, Not Discord

Management and workers should maintain mutual understanding and cooperation rather than conflict.

Both parties should recognize that organizational success benefits the organization and its employees.

**Example:** Management discusses production targets and workplace difficulties with workers instead of imposing unrealistic targets without consultation.

### 3. Cooperation, Not Individualism

Managers and workers should cooperate to ensure that work is performed according to established scientific methods.

Management should provide proper instructions, resources, and support.

**Example:** A production manager trains employees and supplies the necessary tools to help them meet production targets.

### 4. Development of Each Person to Their Greatest Efficiency and Prosperity

Employees should be scientifically selected, properly trained, and assigned work according to their abilities.

The objective is to improve both employee performance and organizational productivity.

**Example:** A company trains workers in operating modern machinery and assigns them jobs suited to their technical skills.

## 2.3 Techniques of Scientific Management

### 1. Time Study

Time study determines the standard time required by a qualified worker to complete a task under specified working conditions.

It involves observing and measuring the time taken to perform different elements of a job.

**Purpose:**
- Establish standard working time.
- Estimate production capacity.
- Improve work scheduling.
- Reduce unnecessary delays.

**Example:** A factory determines that assembling one component should take five minutes under normal working conditions.

### 2. Motion Study

Motion study examines the physical movements required to perform a task. Its purpose is to eliminate unnecessary movements and make work easier and faster.

**Example:** Arranging tools close to a worker reduces the need to walk repeatedly across the factory floor.

### 3. Fatigue Study

Fatigue study examines how working conditions, work duration, and rest periods affect employee tiredness and performance.

It helps determine suitable rest intervals and working conditions.

**Example:** A factory introduces appropriate breaks and ergonomic seating to reduce employee fatigue.

### 4. Method Study

Method study identifies the most effective way to perform a particular job by examining the sequence of activities involved.

**Example:** A packaging company reorganizes its production line so that products move directly from filling to sealing and labeling.

### 5. Standardization

Standardization means establishing uniform standards for tools, equipment, methods, materials, and working conditions.

It reduces unnecessary variations and improves consistency.

**Example:** A manufacturing company uses the same specifications for screws and machine parts throughout its production process.

### 6. Functional Foremanship

Taylor proposed that supervision should be divided among specialized supervisors rather than relying on a single all-purpose foreman.

His original system included eight functional foremen, divided between planning and production functions.

**Planning department:**
- Route clerk: Determines the sequence and route of work.
- Instruction card clerk: Provides instructions about how work should be performed.
- Time and cost clerk: Records time and production costs.
- Disciplinarian: Maintains discipline and addresses rule violations.

**Production department:**
- Gang boss: Ensures machines and tools are ready.
- Speed boss: Ensures the appropriate speed of work.
- Repair boss: Maintains and repairs equipment.
- Inspector: Checks the quality of work.

**Example:** In a factory, one specialist supervises machine maintenance while another checks production quality.

### 7. Differential Piece-Rate System

Taylor introduced a wage incentive system in which workers receive different piece rates depending on whether they meet the prescribed production standard.

- Workers meeting or exceeding the standard receive a higher rate.
- Workers failing to meet the standard receive a lower rate.

**Example:** A factory pays ₹20 per unit to workers meeting the target and ₹15 per unit to workers who fail to meet it.

The system aims to motivate workers to increase productivity. Actual wage systems must comply with applicable labour laws and employment agreements.

## 2.4 Advantages of Scientific Management

1. Increases productivity through improved work methods.
2. Reduces wastage of time, materials, and effort.
3. Establishes clear performance standards.
4. Improves employee training and job allocation.
5. Helps reduce production costs.
6. Encourages cooperation between workers and management.
7. Supports systematic planning and quality control.

## 2.5 Limitations of Scientific Management

1. **Overemphasis on productivity:** Employees may be treated mainly as instruments of production.
2. **Monotony:** Repetitive and highly standardized work can reduce job satisfaction.
3. **Limited attention to social needs:** It does not sufficiently emphasize relationships, emotions, and group behaviour.
4. **Resistance from workers:** Employees may fear increased workloads or job losses.
5. **Limited flexibility:** Standardized procedures may not suit every situation.
6. **Possibility of excessive supervision:** Strict performance measurement can create pressure and dissatisfaction.

## 2.6 Example of Scientific Management in Practice

A car manufacturing company studies the time required for each assembly-line activity. It rearranges tools, eliminates unnecessary movements, standardizes procedures, and trains employees. As a result, production time decreases and output increases.

## 2.7 Important Contributors to the Classical Approach

Although Taylor is associated with Scientific Management, two other contributors are important in the classical school.

### Henri Fayol — Administrative Management

Henri Fayol focused on the management of the entire organization rather than only the efficiency of individual workers.

He proposed management functions and fourteen principles of management.

His principles include division of work, authority and responsibility, discipline, unity of command, unity of direction, and esprit de corps.

**Example:** A company establishes a clear organizational hierarchy, defines departmental responsibilities, and coordinates the work of all departments.

Fayol is widely known as the *Father of Modern Management Theory* or *Administrative Management*.

### Max Weber — Bureaucratic Management

Max Weber developed the theory of bureaucracy, emphasizing formal rules, hierarchy, defined responsibilities, and merit-based appointment.

Important characteristics include:

- Clear division of labour.
- Formal rules and procedures.
- Hierarchical authority.
- Impersonal relationships in official decisions.
- Selection and promotion based on qualifications and competence.
- Written records and documentation.

**Example:** A government department follows established procedures, maintains official records, and recruits employees through merit-based examinations.

### Difference Between Taylor, Fayol, and Weber

| Basis | Taylor | Fayol | Weber |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main focus | Efficiency of individual jobs | Administration of the entire organization | Formal organizational structure |
| Major concern | Worker productivity | Managerial functions and principles | Rules, authority, and hierarchy |
| Approach | Scientific analysis | Administrative principles | Bureaucratic structure |
| Associated theory | Scientific Management | Administrative Management | Bureaucratic Management |

# 3. Human Relations Approach

## 3.1 Meaning and Definition

The Human Relations Approach is a management approach that emphasizes the importance of human needs, social relationships, employee morale, communication, and group behaviour in improving organizational performance.

It argues that employees are not motivated by money alone. Their attitudes, relationships, sense of belonging, and recognition also influence their performance.

The approach is closely associated with **Elton Mayo** and the Hawthorne Studies.

## 3.2 Historical Background

The Human Relations Approach developed mainly during the 1920s and 1930s.

The classical approach concentrated on efficiency, rules, structure, and productivity. However, managers began realizing that employee satisfaction and social relationships could also affect organizational performance.

The Hawthorne Studies at the Western Electric Company's Hawthorne Works in the United States played an important role in the development of this approach.

## 3.3 Hawthorne Studies

The Hawthorne Studies were a series of experiments and observations conducted between 1924 and 1932 at the Western Electric Company's Hawthorne Works near Chicago.

Elton Mayo and other researchers became associated with the interpretation and development of the human relations perspective.

The studies investigated how workplace conditions and social factors were related to employee performance.

### Major Experiments and Observations

#### 1. Illumination Experiments

Researchers studied whether changes in workplace lighting affected workers' productivity.

Productivity sometimes increased under different lighting conditions, making it difficult to explain performance solely through lighting.

**Conclusion:** Physical conditions alone could not fully explain employee behaviour.

#### 2. Relay Assembly Test Room Experiments

A small group of female workers assembled telephone relays under varying working conditions.

Researchers examined factors such as rest pauses, working hours, and supervision.

Changes in productivity were associated with several factors, including group relationships, attention from supervisors, and the working environment.

**Conclusion:** Social and psychological factors may influence productivity alongside physical working conditions.

#### 3. Interviewing Programme

Researchers interviewed employees to understand their attitudes, feelings, and experiences at work.

The interviews highlighted the importance of listening to employees and understanding their concerns.

**Conclusion:** Effective communication and opportunities to express opinions can improve understanding between management and workers.

#### 4. Bank Wiring Observation Room

Researchers observed a group of male workers engaged in wiring work.

The group developed informal production norms and social pressure that influenced individual output.

**Conclusion:** Informal groups and peer expectations can influence employee behaviour, sometimes more strongly than formal incentive systems.

### Hawthorne Effect

The Hawthorne Effect generally refers to a change in people's behaviour when they know they are being observed or given special attention.

**Example:** Employees temporarily improve their work performance because they know that management is monitoring a new workplace programme.

**Important note:** The historical Hawthorne Studies were complex, and later researchers have questioned whether the results can be explained by one single effect. For examination purposes, the term is commonly associated with the influence of observation and attention on behaviour.

## 3.4 Main Features of the Human Relations Approach

1. **Employee-centred management:** Employees are treated as people with individual and social needs.
2. **Importance of social relationships:** Workplace relationships influence morale and performance.
3. **Informal groups:** Unofficial employee groups affect attitudes, behaviour, and productivity.
4. **Communication:** Open communication improves understanding and reduces conflict.
5. **Employee participation:** Involving employees in decisions can increase commitment.
6. **Leadership:** Supportive and considerate leadership can improve morale.
7. **Motivation beyond money:** Recognition, belonging, security, and respect can motivate employees.
8. **Group behaviour:** Group norms and relationships influence individual performance.

## 3.5 Advantages of the Human Relations Approach

1. Improves employee morale and job satisfaction.
2. Encourages better communication.
3. Promotes cooperation and teamwork.
4. Helps managers understand informal groups.
5. Can reduce workplace conflict.
6. Encourages employee participation.
7. Recognizes the psychological and social aspects of work.

## 3.6 Limitations of the Human Relations Approach

1. **Overemphasis on social factors:** Technical, financial, and structural factors may receive insufficient attention.
2. **Limited generalizability:** Social practices that work in one organization may not work equally well in another.
3. **Difficulty in measuring morale:** Employee satisfaction and group relationships can be difficult to measure accurately.
4. **Productivity is not guaranteed:** Satisfied employees do not automatically produce more.
5. **Possible neglect of economic incentives:** Fair pay and material rewards remain important.
6. **Potential manipulation:** Management may use participation or friendly relationships to influence employees without giving them genuine decision-making power.

## 3.7 Example of the Human Relations Approach

A software company notices that employees are dissatisfied despite receiving competitive salaries. Management introduces regular feedback sessions, team meetings, recognition programmes, supportive supervision, and employee participation in decisions. Communication improves and employees develop stronger relationships with colleagues.

# 4. Behavioural Approach to Management

## 4.1 Meaning

The Behavioural Approach extends the Human Relations Approach by applying concepts and research from psychology, sociology, and other behavioural sciences to understand and improve employee behaviour.

It studies motivation, leadership, personality, perception, learning, communication, decision-making, and organizational behaviour.

While the Human Relations Approach highlighted social relationships and morale, the Behavioural Approach developed a broader and more systematic understanding of individual and group behaviour.

## 4.2 Important Contributors

### Abraham Maslow — Hierarchy of Needs

Maslow proposed that human needs can be organized into five broad levels:

1. **Physiological needs:** Food, water, rest, and other basic requirements.
2. **Safety needs:** Physical security, job security, and protection from harm.
3. **Social needs:** Friendship, belonging, and relationships.
4. **Esteem needs:** Respect, recognition, status, and achievement.
5. **Self-actualization needs:** Personal growth and realizing one's potential.

**Example:** An employee may first seek a stable salary and job security, then value teamwork, recognition, and opportunities for professional growth.

The hierarchy is a useful motivational framework, although people do not always pursue needs in a fixed order.

### Douglas McGregor — Theory X and Theory Y

McGregor described two contrasting sets of managerial assumptions about employees.

**Theory X:**
- Employees generally dislike work.
- They require close supervision and control.
- They may avoid responsibility.
- Management relies heavily on rules and external control.

**Theory Y:**
- Work can be natural and meaningful.
- Employees can exercise self-direction.
- People may accept and seek responsibility.
- Creativity and problem-solving abilities exist at many organizational levels.

**Example:** A Theory X manager closely monitors every task, whereas a Theory Y manager establishes goals and allows employees to decide how to achieve them.

These theories describe managerial assumptions, not universal facts about all employees.

### Frederick Herzberg — Two-Factor Theory

Herzberg classified workplace factors into two groups.

**Hygiene factors:**
- Salary.
- Job security.
- Working conditions.
- Company policies.
- Relationships with supervisors.

Poor hygiene factors can cause dissatisfaction, but improving them alone does not necessarily create lasting motivation.

**Motivators:**
- Achievement.
- Recognition.
- Responsibility.
- Advancement.
- Meaningful work.

**Example:** Improving office conditions may reduce dissatisfaction, while giving an employee meaningful responsibility and recognition may increase motivation.

## 4.3 Advantages

1. Provides deeper insight into employee motivation.
2. Supports better leadership and communication.
3. Helps managers understand individual differences.
4. Encourages employee development and participation.
5. Supports conflict resolution and team management.

## 4.4 Limitations

1. Human behaviour is complex and difficult to predict.
2. Different employees respond differently to the same motivational method.
3. Behavioural research may not provide universal solutions.
4. Individual motivation may be influenced by organizational structure, economic conditions, and culture.

# 5. Systems Approach to Management

## 5.1 Meaning and Definition

The Systems Approach views an organization as a system made up of interconnected and interdependent parts that work together to achieve common objectives.

A change in one department can affect other departments and the performance of the entire organization.

The approach emphasizes the relationships between departments and between the organization and its external environment.

## 5.2 Meaning of a System

A system is a collection of interrelated components that interact with one another to achieve a common purpose.

**Example:** A university consists of academic departments, administration, libraries, laboratories, finance, and student services. These components work together to support education and research.

## 5.3 Types of Systems

### 1. Open System

An open system interacts with its external environment by receiving inputs, transforming them, and producing outputs.

Most organizations are open systems because they depend on customers, suppliers, employees, technology, government policies, and financial resources.

**Example:** A manufacturing company purchases raw materials from suppliers, converts them into products, sells them to customers, and uses customer feedback to improve production.

### 2. Closed System

A closed system is treated as having little or no interaction with its external environment.

In practice, completely closed organizational systems are rare. The concept is mainly useful for theoretical analysis.

**Example:** A simplified classroom model that studies a process while temporarily ignoring outside influences can be treated as a closed system.

## 5.4 Major Elements of the Systems Approach

The basic organizational system consists of the following elements:

### 1. Inputs

Inputs are the resources an organization receives from its environment.

Examples:
- Human resources.
- Capital and money.
- Raw materials.
- Information.
- Technology.
- Energy.

### 2. Transformation Process

The transformation process converts inputs into useful outputs through organizational activities.

Examples:
- Manufacturing.
- Teaching.
- Software development.
- Customer service.
- Research and development.

### 3. Outputs

Outputs are the products, services, and results produced by the organization.

Examples:
- Manufactured goods.
- Educational services.
- Software applications.
- Customer satisfaction.
- Profit or other organizational outcomes.

### 4. Feedback

Feedback is information about the results of organizational activities. It helps managers identify problems and make improvements.

**Example:** Customer complaints reveal defects in a product, leading the company to improve its manufacturing process.

### 5. Environment

The environment includes external forces that affect the organization.

Examples:
- Customers.
- Competitors.
- Suppliers.
- Government regulations.
- Economic conditions.
- Technological developments.

### 6. Boundaries

Boundaries separate an organization from its environment while allowing necessary interactions.

For example, an organization has its own employees, policies, and internal processes, but it also interacts with customers, suppliers, and regulators.

### 7. Subsystems

Subsystems are smaller systems within a larger system.

Examples:
- Finance.
- Marketing.
- Human resources.
- Production.
- Information technology.

Each subsystem has its own responsibilities but depends on the other subsystems.

## 5.5 Input–Transformation–Output Model

A basic systems model can be represented as:

**Inputs → Transformation Process → Outputs → Feedback**

The environment influences the process, and feedback helps the organization adjust its inputs and activities.

**Example: A Bakery**

- Inputs: Flour, sugar, employees, ovens, and money.
- Transformation: Mixing, baking, packaging, and quality checking.
- Outputs: Bread, cakes, and customer services.
- Feedback: Customer reviews and sales data.
- Improvement: Changes in recipes, quality, production quantities, or pricing.

## 5.6 Important Concepts in the Systems Approach

### Interdependence

Interdependence means that different departments and components rely on one another.

**Example:** The marketing department depends on production to supply the products promised to customers.

### Synergy

Synergy occurs when the combined performance of components produces a result greater than what they could achieve independently.

**Example:** Coordinated work between designers, developers, and testers may produce better software than each team working in isolation.

### Holism

Holism means understanding the organization as a whole rather than studying each department in isolation.

**Example:** A company should evaluate the combined effect of its marketing, production, finance, and customer service decisions.

### Feedback and Adaptation

Organizations use information about performance to correct errors and respond to changes.

**Example:** A decline in sales prompts a company to investigate customer needs and modify its products.

## 5.7 Advantages of the Systems Approach

1. Encourages a complete view of the organization.
2. Improves coordination among departments.
3. Highlights interdependence between organizational activities.
4. Recognizes the importance of external environmental factors.
5. Encourages feedback and continuous improvement.
6. Helps managers understand the wider consequences of decisions.
7. Supports long-term planning and adaptation.

## 5.8 Limitations of the Systems Approach

1. It may be too broad and abstract for some practical decisions.
2. Identifying all relevant relationships can be difficult.
3. It may not provide a specific solution to a particular management problem.
4. Collecting and analysing system-wide information can be costly.
5. Changes in one part of the organization may have unpredictable consequences.

# 6. Contingency Approach to Management

## 6.1 Meaning and Definition

The Contingency Approach states that there is no single management method that is best for every organization or situation.

The appropriate management practice depends on the circumstances, including the nature of the task, employee skills, organizational size, technology, and external environment.

In simple words, **the best management method depends on the situation.**

The approach developed as management scholars recognized that the effectiveness of a management technique varies according to organizational conditions.

## 6.2 Main Features of the Contingency Approach

### 1. No Universal Solution

A management method that works in one situation may fail in another.

**Example:** Strict procedures may be suitable for a safety-critical production line, while a software design team may benefit from greater flexibility.

### 2. Situation-Based Decision-Making

Managers should examine the conditions before selecting a management method.

**Example:** During an emergency, a manager may need to make quick decisions. During routine planning, employee consultation may be more effective.

### 3. Flexibility

Managers should adapt their leadership, organizational structure, and decision-making methods to changing conditions.

**Example:** A company changes its working arrangements when it moves from a small startup structure to a large multinational structure.

### 4. Importance of Environmental Factors

The external environment influences which management practices are appropriate.

**Example:** A business facing intense competition may need faster decision-making than a business operating in a stable market.

### 5. Relationship Between Variables

The approach studies how factors such as technology, task complexity, employee competence, organizational size, and uncertainty influence management effectiveness.

## 6.3 Major Contingency Factors

### 1. Organizational Size

Small organizations may function effectively with informal communication and simple structures. Large organizations often need specialized departments and formal procedures.

### 2. Technology

Different technologies require different management practices.

**Example:** An automated factory requires technical monitoring and maintenance, whereas a creative design agency may require collaboration and flexible work processes.

### 3. External Environment

Stable environments may permit long-term planning and formal procedures. Rapidly changing environments may require flexibility and quicker decisions.

### 4. Employee Skills and Experience

Experienced employees may perform effectively with greater autonomy, while inexperienced employees may require more guidance and training.

### 5. Nature of the Task

Routine tasks may benefit from standardized procedures. Complex and innovative tasks may require experimentation and employee participation.

### 6. Organizational Culture

Management practices must consider shared values, workplace expectations, and employee behaviour.

## 6.4 Example of the Contingency Approach

A manager supervises two teams.

- **Team A:** Employees operate industrial machinery where safety and consistency are essential. The manager establishes clear procedures, close monitoring, and defined responsibilities.
- **Team B:** Software developers are creating a new product. The manager encourages experimentation, team discussion, and flexible problem-solving.

The manager uses different methods because the tasks and working conditions are different.

This illustrates the contingency principle that management practices should fit the situation.

## 6.5 Advantages of the Contingency Approach

1. Encourages flexible decision-making.
2. Recognizes differences between organizations.
3. Helps managers respond to changing conditions.
4. Avoids blindly applying universal rules.
5. Supports situation-specific leadership and organizational design.
6. Encourages analysis of multiple factors before making decisions.

## 6.6 Limitations of the Contingency Approach

1. Managers may find it difficult to identify all relevant factors.
2. Analysing each situation can consume time and resources.
3. It does not provide one simple set of rules for every problem.
4. Managers require experience and judgement to choose suitable methods.
5. Rapidly changing situations can make decisions more difficult.

## 6.7 Important Contributors

Several scholars contributed to contingency thinking. Important names include:

- **Fred Fiedler:** Developed the contingency model of leadership.
- **Paul Lawrence and Jay Lorsch:** Studied organizational differentiation, integration, and environmental conditions.
- **Joan Woodward:** Studied relationships between production technology and organizational structure.
- **Tom Burns and G. M. Stalker:** Examined mechanistic and organic organizational forms in different environments.

These contributions show that organizational design and leadership effectiveness depend partly on situational conditions.

|---|---|---|

# 10. Overall Importance of the Evolution of Management

The evolution of management has improved how organizations plan, organize, lead, and control their activities.

Its major contributions include:

1. **Improved efficiency:** Scientific methods help reduce waste and increase productivity.
2. **Better employee management:** Human Relations and Behavioural approaches emphasize motivation and workplace relationships.
3. **Improved coordination:** Systems thinking encourages cooperation among departments.
4. **Greater adaptability:** Contingency thinking helps managers respond to changing circumstances.
5. **Better decision-making:** Modern management combines observation, data, employee feedback, and situational analysis.
6. **Balanced organizational performance:** Effective management considers productivity, employee well-being, quality, customer satisfaction, and long-term sustainability.

Modern organizations rarely depend on only one approach. They combine methods according to their objectives, people, technology, and operating environment.

**Example:** A hospital may standardize surgical procedures for safety, use human relations principles to improve staff teamwork, coordinate departments as an integrated system, and adapt its staffing arrangements according to patient demand.

# 12. Model Long Answer: Explain the Evolution of Management

## Introduction

Management has evolved gradually in response to changes in industrial development, organizational size, technology, and employee expectations. Early management practices concentrated on discipline and productivity, whereas modern approaches consider employee behaviour, organizational interdependence, and environmental uncertainty.

## 1. Classical Approach

The Classical Approach emphasized efficiency, formal structure, specialization, and organizational discipline.

It includes three important branches:

- **Scientific Management:** F. W. Taylor focused on improving the efficiency of individual jobs through scientific methods, standardization, and training.
- **Administrative Management:** Henri Fayol focused on managerial functions and principles for managing the entire organization.
- **Bureaucratic Management:** Max Weber emphasized formal rules, hierarchy, defined authority, and merit-based selection.

The classical approach helped establish systematic management practices, although it gave relatively limited attention to employees' social and psychological needs.

## 2. Human Relations Approach

The Human Relations Approach developed as researchers recognized the importance of employee morale, social relationships, communication, and informal groups.

Elton Mayo and the Hawthorne Studies are closely associated with this approach.

It demonstrated the importance of understanding employees as social individuals rather than considering only their physical output.

## 3. Behavioural Approach

The Behavioural Approach applied psychology and sociology to the study of employee motivation, leadership, and behaviour.

Abraham Maslow's hierarchy of needs, Douglas McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y, and Frederick Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory are important contributions.

This approach helped managers understand why different employees may respond differently to workplace conditions and motivational practices.

## 4. Systems Approach

The Systems Approach views an organization as a collection of interdependent components.

It explains how inputs are transformed into outputs and how feedback helps an organization improve its performance.

The approach emphasizes coordination between departments and interaction with the external environment.

## 5. Contingency Approach

The Contingency Approach argues that no single management technique is suitable for every situation.

Managers should consider factors such as organizational size, technology, employee skills, task complexity, and environmental uncertainty before choosing a management method.

It emphasizes flexibility and situational decision-making.

## Conclusion

The evolution of management reflects a shift from a narrow focus on production efficiency toward a broader understanding of people, organizational systems, and environmental conditions. Each approach contributes useful ideas, and effective managers combine them according to the needs of their organizations.
